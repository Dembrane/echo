# Inside the Millbrook Heat Transition: Real-World Friction, Red Lines, and the Battle for a Just 2035 Plan

The Millbrook Citizens' Assembly brought together hundreds of local residents, tradespeople, and civic leaders to deliberate on the mandated retirement of the town's fossil gas network by 2035. Rather than rubber-stamping standard technology roadmaps, the assembly's deliberations revealed sharp structural fractures across housing tenures, technical infrastructure limits, and deep-seated civic distrust. This interview-style report examines the core debates, non-negotiable community boundaries, and proposed solutions that emerged from across the district's neighborhoods.


### Why has the proposed 2035 gas retirement deadline generated such intense controversy between residents and local officials?

The debate over the 2035 deadline exposed a fundamental division regarding risk and equity. Council officials clarified that while national policy frameworks set a fossil gas phase-out between 2035 and 2040, Millbrook’s local climate emergency declaration committed the district to the earlier 2035 target. Homeowners and skeptics argued that rushing to hit an arbitrary local date forces residents to shoulder a severe "guinea-pig premium," retiring functional heating systems while supply chains and equipment costs remain at their peak. Conversely, low-income renters and climate advocates countered that delaying the phase-out to 2040 creates an equally dangerous economic trap: as affluent homeowners transition early to clean technologies, the compounding fixed costs of maintaining a dying gas grid will fall entirely on low-income households unable to afford the upfront transition. Ultimately, participants insisted that no gas line be disconnected without a binding 100 percent street-completion guarantee and non-debt funding bridges for vulnerable residents.


### Why did participants across every session insist that building insulation must precede any heating system upgrades?

The assembly reached complete consensus on the principle of "insulation first," memorably described by participants as refusing to pour tea into a cracked cup. Testimonies revealed that installing low-temperature systems, such as air-source heat pumps, into Millbrook's uninsulated 1930s terraces or drafty rental conversions leads to immediate system failure, leaving rooms freezing and driving electric bills to catastrophic levels when emergency immersion heaters engage. In practical renovations, residents demonstrated that addressing building fabric through cavity wall insulation, draft-proofing, and double glazing halved energy consumption prior to altering the heating plant, enabling the subsequent installation of smaller, less costly heat pumps. Consequently, participants demanded that mandatory thermal efficiency standards be certified as the prerequisite step in every transition pathway, rather than treated as an optional companion measure.


### How did the assembly propose resolving the split-incentive deadlock between landlords and private tenants?

The relationship between private landlords and tenants emerged as one of the most volatile friction points in Millbrook, particularly in dense conversion areas like Weaver's Court. Landlords emphasized that an average heat pump retrofit costs between €12,000 and €18,000, a capital expenditure that delivers bill savings exclusively to tenants while leaving property owners with debt and lost rental yield. Tenants and youth representatives, however, warned that imposing retrofit mandates without strict legal safeguards would trigger widespread "green renovictions," where landlords issue Section 21 no-fault evictions to remodel properties and re-let them to wealthier commuters at substantial markups. To bridge this divide, the assembly formulated a "warm rent guarantee": landlords receive a 60 percent address-linked public capital subsidy on the condition that they enter into a legally binding seven-year rent freeze, where a tenant's combined rent and heating costs cannot increase, accompanied by strict bans on retaliatory evictions.


### What structural barriers prevent multi-occupancy, mixed-tenure tower blocks like Birch Court from undergoing standard retrofits?

Discussions surrounding Birch Court, a 1974 fourteen-storey municipal high-rise, demonstrated that mixed-tenure blocks are legally and physically paralyzed under existing frameworks. Social tenants currently suffer under uncontrollable, calendar-dictated heating seasons and uniform flat charges, while private leaseholders face catastrophic, uncapped Section 20 major works bills exceeding €14,000 per flat because communal systems are excluded from standard individual homeowner grants. Technically, Birch Court’s fifty-year-old single-pipe vertical heating risers cannot handle the flow dynamics of modern low-temperature heat pumps without risking structural leaks and heating failures on upper floors. The assembly rejected top-down municipal interventions, demanding an independent engineering overhaul that utilizes external stairwell risers to avoid gutting individual bathrooms, individual digital sub-metering for all 84 units, grant parity for leaseholders matched to individual homeowner subsidies, and a joint tenant-leaseholder works board with legal veto power over contractor procurement and energy tariffs.


### Why do rural and off-grid communities view the council’s electrification plan as an active safety hazard?

Residents living in outlying rural pockets such as Blackwood Dike, High Moor Lane, and Whinney Ridge challenged the premise of the assembly, pointing out that mains gas infrastructure never reached their properties in the first place. These communities rely on bulk LPG, heating oil, and seasoned timber, and endure multi-day winter power outages caused by fragile overhead single-phase power lines. Rural participants argued that an outright ban on fossil and solid fuels without massive prior grid reinforcement would leave isolated households without heating, water pumps, or livestock support during severe winter storms. Furthermore, standard grant schemes offering €3,000 vouchers were deemed useless against off-grid conversion estimates exceeding €24,000, which frequently include mandatory €8,000 pole-top transformer upgrade fees demanded by the network operator. As an absolute red line, off-grid residents demanded that the right to retain independent, non-electric secondary heating, such as multifuel stoves, be legally protected.


### What unique challenges do mixed-use, live-work properties on the High Street face during the transition?

Shopkeepers living in flats above their commercial premises, including bakers, hairdressers, and café owners, highlighted that standard domestic decarbonisation policies ignore mixed-use buildings. High Street properties feature continuous shopfronts with zero outdoor yard space for bulky heat pump condensers, while alleyways serve as narrow, shared fire escapes where mechanical equipment cannot legally be installed. Furthermore, commercial operations such as hair salons require continuous 40-degree water delivery that domestic hot water cylinders cannot sustain, and commercial bakeries rely on high-capacity gas baking decks that would trip the 1950s single-phase electrical supply along the commercial spine. Business owners warned that disruptive street trenching during peak retail months would devastate footfall and destroy trade margins. Consequently, they demanded a unified domestic-commercial grant category, full public funding for three-phase power upgrades, and legally guaranteed daily compensation for lost trading during civil infrastructure works.


### How do strict heritage conservation policies undermine heating improvements in areas like Church Lane?

In designated historic districts like Church Lane, property owners face an irreconcilable conflict between local decarbonisation targets and punitive planning rules. Residents living in two-hundred-year-old stone cottages reported that planning officers routinely reject applications for slimline vacuum double-glazing and external heat pumps, citing "aesthetic harm" to the setting of historic churches, while leaving occupants to endure damp, mold, and monthly gas bills exceeding €400. Surveyors pointed out that individual air-source units would generate intolerable acoustic reverberations within narrow medieval stone courtyards, while standard internal wall insulation risks trapping moisture and causing catastrophic structural decay in historic lime-mortar masonry. To resolve this impasse, the assembly called for a statutory presumption in favor of decarbonisation within conservation bylaws, fee-free listed building retrofit applications, twenty-year municipal guarantees against interstitial moisture damage, and the construction of a shared ground-source array beneath the parish green governed as a community-owned utility.


### What acute clinical risks does the heating transition pose for care facilities and medically vulnerable households?

Testimonies from care home managers at Rowan House and families supporting individuals with advanced disabilities revealed that domestic energy-saving assumptions, such as lowering thermostats to 18°C or wearing extra clothing, represent acute, life-threatening medical hazards. Medically vulnerable residents, such as individuals with autonomic nervous system damage or quadriplegia, require constant ambient temperatures of 21°C to 23°C to prevent immediate hospitalisation from hypothermia and respiratory decline. Furthermore, care home operators warned that decanting frail residents with advanced dementia to facilitate deep internal retrofits causes severe transfer trauma and elevated mortality. For households reliant on continuous medical equipment like profiling beds and oxygen concentrators, participants demanded fully funded 10-to-15 kWh failover batteries with automatic grid islanding, mandatory two-hour physical emergency callouts during winter power cuts, and an at-cost Medical Energy Lifeline Tariff to eliminate the financial penalty on life-support electricity.


### What workforce shortages and electrical supply limits represent the primary delivery bottlenecks for Millbrook?

A dedicated panel of local tradespeople delivered a sobering assessment of the local supply chain, stating that the district's installer capacity is near total collapse. Millbrook currently has only two installers certified to install heat pumps under the Microgeneration Certification Scheme (MCS), alongside approximately eighteen Gas Safe engineers, several of whom are approaching retirement and unwilling to absorb thousands of euros in uncompensated downtime to retrain. Technicians noted that while replacing a gas combi boiler requires a single day, an effective heat pump retrofit demands four to five days of extensive pipework resizing, hydronic balancing, and electrical upgrades. On the electrical supply side, older residential streets still rely on outdated 60-amp incoming service cutouts that cannot support simultaneous heat pump loads, car chargers, and induction hobs without blowing local substation feeders. The trades demanded that local capacity be treated as critical civil infrastructure, requiring full wage-replacement compensation for sole-trader retraining, a municipal hardware supply hub, and independent commissioning checks before public grants are disbursed.


### Why did participants express profound distrust toward private district heat networks and municipal digital administration?

Distrust surfaced repeatedly throughout the assembly, driven by residents' negative experiences with both privatized utilities and automated council systems. Participants pointed to contemporary developments like the Verwey network, where residents locked into private district heating monopolies suffered sudden 40 percent tariff spikes in midwinter with no regulatory oversight and no ability to switch suppliers. Concurrently, older and digitally excluded residents reported immense emotional distress trying to navigate multi-agency online portals, automated phone queues, and uncoordinated official letters following family bereavements. In response, the assembly firmly rejected privatized heat concessions, demanding municipal or community ownership with statutory price caps for any shared network. To re-establish administrative trust, participants overwhelmingly favored human-led communication over digital portals, championing volunteer-led "Heat Buddies," door-to-door welfare checks, and face-to-face neighborhood hubs situated in trusted civic spaces like post offices, church halls, and local markets.


To achieve an equitable, viable 2035 heating transition, the Millbrook Citizens' Assembly demonstrated that technical targets cannot be separated from the lived realities of housing tenure, grid infrastructure, and human vulnerability. Moving forward into concrete policy development, the assembly established that the transition must abandon one-size-fits-all mandates in favor of a sequenced, street-by-street approach that guarantees thermal insulation first, underwrites financial risks for early adopters, and protects residents from eviction and fuel poverty through binding legal covenants.
